Write it the way the narration of a great nature documentary is written: for the EAR, not for the page. Nobody will read this text; one voice will say it once, slowly, over images.

Shape of the chapter (a small story, not a lesson):
- Open inside a moment: a place, a person looking at something, a strange fact, a quiet observation. Within the first few sentences, let the chapter's mystery appear as a question the listener now wants answered.
- Climb toward the answer in two or three steps: a clue, then how someone found out, then what it really means. Each step should make the next one feel necessary.
- Land the reveal plainly and briefly. Then give it room: one or two calm sentences on what it means, ideally for the listener, for Earth, or for us as a species.
- Leave a thread for the next chapter, without announcing it.

The reveal lines (very important, the editing and sound are built on them):
- Two to four times in the chapter, put a key moment ALONE as its own paragraph: one short sentence, at most twelve words, with a blank line before and after it. The narrator pauses before these lines and the music breathes around them.
- Use them for the payoff of a build-up ("It was raining. Methane, on another world."), a turn ("And yet, the rings are vanishing."), or a quiet image that sums up a moment. Never two in a row, never as the very first line.

Sentences that sound spoken:
- One idea per sentence. Subject and verb early. No chains of subordinate clauses, no long asides between commas.
- Vary the rhythm on purpose: after a long, flowing sentence, a short one. Most sentences between 8 and 22 words; never more than 30.
- Make every big number felt with one human-scale comparison (a walk, a lifetime, a grain of sand, the distance between two cities). Never stack several numbers in one sentence.
- Use contrast to create gentle tension: what everyone expected, and what was actually there.
- Concrete senses over abstractions: what you would see, hear, feel underfoot, how cold, how quiet.
- Callbacks are welcome: briefly echo an image from an earlier chapter when it connects.
- Avoid the clichés of AI documentary writing, in any language: "the vastness of the cosmos", "cosmic dance", "tapestry", "symphony", "silent witness", "a testament to", "invites us to reflect", "majestic", "breathtaking", "mesmerizing", "journey through" more than once, "Imagine" (not even once in this chapter). Say the specific thing instead.

Accuracy and calm:
- Teach something real and specific in every paragraph. Accuracy beats drama. If a value is uncertain, say "about" or "roughly"; if science does not know, say so — an honest open question is a good ending for a paragraph.
- No hype, no cliffhangers, no exclamations: the listener may be falling asleep. The tension is curiosity, never alarm.
